> **Update, November 7, 2026.** We first described each EDR's stop rate as the share of attacks the EDR stopped. It is really the share stopped on hosts running that EDR, and some of those stops were Windows itself. A credential-dumping stage was refused access to LSASS on hosts of all four EDRs with no sign of any EDR, which points to Windows LSA protection. The new section 3 gives both figures. The EDR on top doesn't change. We found this by checking the study against the Project Achilles dashboards.

## TL;DR

- For 30 days, Project Achilles ran six multi-stage attack chains, built from published threat intelligence, on 27 Windows endpoints of one organization. Each endpoint had one of four EDR products in active mode. We call them EDR A to D.
- Each EDR raised an alert on 81% to 100% of the runs on its hosts. The share of attacks stopped on those hosts ranged from 30% to 90%. The share the EDR provably stopped itself ranged from 4% to 76%.
- The EDR that stopped the most did it mainly by blocking test binaries before they ran. That is real protection, but it also means we never saw how it handles the later stages of an attack.
- One EDR, with every agent reporting "protect" mode, stopped 9% of the attack stages that ran, and Windows, not the EDR, blocked most of those. It raised alerts on many more.
- Three techniques got past every EDR that faced them: masquerading (T1036), exfiltration over the command-and-control channel (T1041) and credential theft from password stores (T1555).
- Five differences between EDRs looked statistically solid until we dropped a single test. Then they nearly vanished. One snapshot is not a measurement.

## Why We Ran This

In [Your Defenses Look Green. Are They Working?](/posts/2026-11-05-your-defenses-look-green-are-they-working) we described how continuous validation works, using illustrative data. This post shows what it found on a real fleet over a month.

We had an unusual setup to work with. The organization runs four different EDR products, each one active on a different group of Windows endpoints. On 13 of the 27 endpoints, a second EDR product also runs in passive mode. Over 30 days, Project Achilles ran the same six attack chains across all of them:

| Attack chain | What it does |
|---|---|
| Kothamine Tailcat | Encrypted command-and-control channel and data exfiltration |
| LockBit 3.0 | Double-extortion ransomware kill chain |
| Qilin (Agenda) | Vulnerable-driver (BYOVD) EDR killer, then injection into `svchost` |
| Lumma-style infostealer | Browser credential theft through process injection |
| The Gentlemen | GPO-driven, living-off-the-land intrusion |
| Star Blizzard | RedFlick/CosmicPulse espionage chain |

That gave us 177 runs we could analyze, each one split into its stages, plus every alert the four EDRs raised on those hosts during the period.

We don't name the products. Several EDR licenses restrict publishing comparative results. More importantly, most of what we found comes down to configuration and test design, not brand. Treat the letters as four real deployments, not as a product ranking.

## 1. Almost Everything Was Seen. Far Less Was Stopped.

![For each EDR, the share of runs on which it raised an alert or a prevention, against the share of attacks it stopped. EDR A: 100% versus 48%. EDR B: 91% versus 90%. EDR C: 81% versus 48%. EDR D: 94% versus 30%.](/edr-study-30-days/seen-vs-stopped.png)

Every EDR raised something on most runs: between 81% and 100% of them. Measured by visibility, they all look good. But in three of the four, there is a gap of 33 to 64 percentage points between "raised an alert" and "stopped the attack".

That gap is not a failure of the dashboard. The dashboard does what it was built to do: it shows that the sensor is running and producing alerts. It does not show whether the attack completed, and an attack whose every stage raises an alert can still reach its objective. A detection only helps if someone acts on it in time.

## 2. Where an EDR Stops an Attack Decides What You Can Learn

![For each EDR, how its runs ended. EDR B blocked 54% at launch and stopped 36% during the chain. EDR A stopped 48% during the chain and saw the other 52% without stopping them. EDR C stopped 48%, saw 37% without stopping them, and 15% of its runs ended in a test error. EDR D stopped 30% and saw 68% without stopping them.](/edr-study-30-days/where-stopped.png)

EDR B stopped 90% of attacks, by far the most. More than half of those stops came from blocking the test binary at launch, before any technique ran. That is genuine protection: attackers use fresh binaries too, and refusing to run them is a strong defense.

It also has a side effect. On three of the six attack chains, EDR B's hosts produced no usable stage result at all. So we know EDR B blocks those binaries. We don't know how it would handle the in-memory and living-off-the-land stages if a binary did run, for example one delivered in a way its launch checks don't cover. On the three chains where stages did run, EDR B stopped or detected 54% of them.

The way to fill that gap is a test variant the EDR doesn't stop at launch, such as a chain started from a script or a built-in Windows tool, with the production policy left exactly as it is. Never weaken a protection to make it measurable.

## 3. Who Did the Stopping?

A test can tell that a stage was blocked. It can't always tell what blocked it. In the first version of this study, we counted every stop on a host as a stop by the EDR active on that host. When we went back to the per-stage evidence, 36 stops had no EDR evidence on the blocked stage: no EDR flagged a prevention there, and the active EDR raised no alert on it.

Most of them were the same stage. A credential-dumping step asks Windows for a handle to read LSASS memory, with debug privileges enabled. On hosts of all four EDRs, Windows refused. The refusal looks the same under four different EDR products and carries no EDR evidence, which points to Windows LSA protection: LSASS running as a protected process. That's a real control doing its job. It just isn't the EDR.

Counting only the stops each EDR proved itself (a launch block or an agent kill matched to its own alert, or its own prevention flag on the blocked stage):

| EDR | Attacks stopped on its hosts | Stopped by the EDR, with its own evidence |
|---|---|---|
| EDR A | 48% | 38% |
| EDR B | 90% | 76% |
| EDR C | 48% | 33% |
| EDR D | 30% | 4% |

EDR B still leads, and its interval doesn't overlap any of the others'. Windows did most of EDR D's stopping. For EDR C, the second figure is a floor: many of its blocks raise no alert, so they leave no evidence to count.

Before you give an EDR credit for a stop, or decide which control to keep paying for, find out which control did it. Here, most of the stops credited to the EDRs without evidence came from a Windows setting.

## 4. "Protect Mode" Is Not a Guarantee

Every EDR D agent reported that it was in protect mode. EDR D still stopped only 30% of attacks on its hosts and 9% of the attack stages that ran, and section 3 shows that Windows did most of that stopping. In 68% of its runs, it raised alerts and let the chain continue.

A likely cause: modern EDRs have several detection engines (behavioral rules, custom rules, cloud analytics), and each engine has its own response setting. The agent can be in protect mode while the engines that actually fired are set to alert only. Check this engine by engine, not agent by agent.

EDR A shows a different pattern. It detected every run and stopped 48%, but all-or-nothing by attack chain. It stopped every Kothamine run and none of the Lumma, Gentlemen or Star Blizzard runs, while raising alerts on all of them. Its policy blocks some kinds of behavior and only reports others.

## 5. Alert Time Is Not Block Time

Median time from the start of a run to the EDR's first alert:

| EDR | Median time to first alert | Runs with an alert |
|---|---|---|
| EDR A | 24 s | 30 |
| EDR B | 64 s | 41 |
| EDR D | 73 s | 34 |
| EDR C | 2.3 min | 46 |

These numbers measure how fast the alert reaches the console, not how fast the attack was blocked. EDR B blocks binaries at launch, within the first second, yet its alerts for those blocks appeared about a minute later. For your SOC, alert time is what matters: it's how long before anyone can respond. For the attack, block time is what matters. Measure them separately, and be suspicious of any vendor figure that doesn't say which one it is.

One more thing worth measuring: the ATT&CK technique on the alert matched the technique the test actually performed in only 14% to 49% of alerts, depending on the EDR. If your SOC triages or reports by ATT&CK technique, it's working from labels that are often wrong.

## 6. A Second Sensor Helps Some Groups and Not Others

![On the hosts of three EDRs, how each executed attack stage was seen by the active EDR and by the second EDR product running in passive mode. On EDR A hosts, the passive sensor alone flagged 4% of stages. On EDR B hosts, it alone flagged 19%. On EDR D hosts, it alone flagged 17%.](/edr-study-30-days/second-sensor.png)

On 13 hosts, a second EDR product ran in passive mode next to the active one, so both watched exactly the same attacks. How much the second sensor added depended entirely on its partner:

- On **EDR B** hosts, the passive sensor raised alerts on 16 stages that EDR B did not, against 10 the other way round.
- On **EDR D** hosts, it was nearly even: 23 against 28.
- On **EDR A** hosts, it added almost nothing: 4 against 30.

Some of those stages may have been stopped by the active EDR without an alert, so this measures visibility, not protection. It still answers a common licensing question with data instead of opinion: a second sensor is worth what it adds on your hosts, next to your primary EDR, and that can be measured.

## 7. Three Techniques Nobody Caught

Three techniques were missed in at least 80% of their stages by every EDR that faced them:

- **T1036, Masquerading**: binaries disguised as legitimate programs.
- **T1041, Exfiltration over the C2 channel**: stolen data leaving through the attacker's own connection.
- **T1555, Credentials from password stores**: harvesting saved credentials.

These aren't reasons to pick one EDR over another, because none of them caught these. They are a detection-engineering backlog: custom rules, network monitoring for data leaving through C2, and hardening of credential stores. You only find a backlog like this by running the techniques.

## How Sure Can We Be?

With 4 to 14 hosts per EDR, it's easy to see a pattern that isn't there. We treated the host, not the run, as the unit of analysis, and gave every comparison a confidence label based on a bootstrap over hosts. A difference is labelled **clear** only if all of these hold:

- the 95% interval excludes zero,
- each EDR has at least three hosts,
- the difference holds when any single host is left out,
- the difference holds when any single attack chain is left out,
- the comparison rests on at least three attack chains both EDRs faced.

**What held up.** EDR B stopped 42 to 60 percentage points more attacks than each of the other three. That difference is clear and survives removing any host or any test. It also holds when only each EDR's own evidence counts (section 3). EDR A and EDR C stopped the same share of attacks (48% each), so there's no winner there.

**What didn't.** Five differences passed every check except the last one:

| Difference | Gap | Without that test |
|---|---|---|
| EDR C stops more attacks than EDR D | +18 pp | +6 pp without Lumma |
| EDR A stops more attacks than EDR D | +18 pp | +2 pp without Kothamine |
| EDR A stops more stages than EDR D | +22 pp | +3 pp without Kothamine |
| EDR C sees more stages than EDR B | +14 pp | +1 pp without LockBit |
| EDR A sees more stages than EDR C | +12 pp | +2 pp without Kothamine |

Each one is real, but it's about one attack chain, not the EDR in general. "EDR A sees more than EDR C" really means "EDR A handled Kothamine much better", which is more specific and more useful to act on.

Three more effects make single measurements unreliable:

- **The same test on the same host doesn't always give the same result.** Across repeated runs, the outcome was identical only 50% to 75% of the time, depending on the EDR. One run tells you little.
- **Test binaries change.** The attack chains were rebuilt during the month, and not every EDR saw every build. Recomputing on only the build every EDR saw (five of the six chains) gives stop rates of 58% for A, 90% for B, 57% for C and 38% for D. EDR B still leads, but by less.
- **Integrations start at different times.** One EDR's alert data only covered the last eight days of the period, so its earlier runs were left out instead of being counted as misses.

## What This Means for Your Environment

None of these findings appear in an EDR console, and most wouldn't appear in an annual penetration test either: one run, on one build, on a handful of hosts, can't distinguish "this EDR is better" from "this EDR did better on this test". That's the case for continuous validation in practice:

1. **Measure stopped separately from seen.** An alert on every stage of an attack that completed is not protection.
2. **Find out which control did the stopping.** An exit code says a stage was blocked, not what blocked it. Give an EDR credit only for the stops it can prove.
3. **Check response settings per detection engine**, not per agent.
4. **Measure alert latency separately from block time.**
5. **Run the techniques nobody blocks**, and turn the misses into detection rules.
6. **Repeat across hosts and over time** before deciding anything, and keep a confidence label next to every comparison.
7. **If you pay for a second sensor, measure what it adds** on each group of hosts.
